First pass

Wall moisture

Will Exterior Wall 1 stay dry enough to prevent mold?

We simulated five years of heat and moisture moving through Exterior Wall 1 (SD-500), hour by hour, in WUFI Pro. We ran it with the cedar siding and with the stone cladding at SD-501 detail FE3, and judged each run against the mold limit in ASHRAE Standard 160. These are first-pass results, pending our internal review.

Passes

Cedar siding

1.3 highest mold index as designed; the limit is 3

Stays well below the limit in every weather year, orientation and indoor moisture level we tested. It fails only if rain keeps leaking through the ZIP-R seams or flashing. 25 of 27 runs pass.

Fails

Stone as drawn (FE3)

5.2 highest mold index as designed; the limit is 3

Fails in all 22 runs, even with no rain leakage. The wet mortar bed sits directly on the ZIP-R and holds the sheathing at about 99% relative humidity all year.

Passes

Stone on a drainage mat

1.8 highest mold index as designed; the limit is 3

Passes in every weather year and with every stone type and color we tested. It tolerates less water at the sheathing than the cedar wall, so laps and flashing matter more.

Recommendations

  1. Put a second water-resistive barrier and a drained space behind the stone. ZIP-R counts only as the first barrier. Huber requires at least one more, mechanically fastened, before the lath and stone (Huber manual, p. 8); its adhered stone detail calls for one meeting ASTM E2556 (p. 39). Huber prohibits a self-adhered or fluid-applied membrane over the whole ZIP-R wall (Huber manual, p. 7). Huber reads the 2021 IBC as also requiring a drainage space of at least 3/16 in in moist climates such as Rye's (Huber tech tip HUB 9213); confirm the requirement under the 2025 NYS Building Code with the code official. Two ways to get both:
    • DELTA-DRY & LATH, which we modeled, over a mechanically fastened barrier on the ZIP-R. It is a dimpled plastic sheet with a drained gap and built-in lath for the scratch coat. Dörken installs it outboard of a water-resistive barrier, so it doesn't replace the second barrier.
    • Huber's ZIP System Rainscreen: a 1/4 in drainage mat whose vapor-permeable fabric face, with its vertical seams taped, serves as the second barrier (Rainscreen manual, pp. 4 and 5). The stone still needs lath (p. 6). Its fabric passes far more vapor than the DELTA-DRY sheet, so ask us to rerun the stone wall before choosing it.
  2. Detail every place water could get past the ZIP-R. Seam tape, window and door flashing and penetrations are where the cedar wall can fail. Behind the stone, also detail the mat laps, its top edge and the weep screed at the base. In our runs, rain that reached the ZIP-R behind the mat pushed the mold index past the limit even with the gap vented. Where flashing seals to the ZIP-R, use Huber's own tape or liquid flash; Huber warrants the adhesion of only those on its panels (Huber manual, p. 6).
  3. Keep the sheathing and framing dry during construction. Install the wood fiber dry and close in the ZIP-R before it gets wet. In our runs the stone wall built wet failed even on the mat, so don't install the mat and stone until the ZIP-R is dry. Cover the ZIP-R with cladding within 180 days; after that, Huber requires a mechanically fastened barrier over it (Huber manual, p. 7).
  4. Vent the mat at the top if the detail allows. We modeled a gap vented top and bottom at 5 air changes an hour, against 1 in the base case. That roughly halved the peak mold index at the ZIP-R, from about 1.5 to 0.7. The wall passes without it.

ZIP-R limits to design around

From Huber's ZIP-R installation manual and the product's evaluation report, ICC-ES ESR-3373.

  • Walls only, no roofs. Huber doesn't warrant or recommend ZIP-R on roofs (Huber manual, p. 7), and ESR-3373 evaluates it only for walls (section 2.0). The roof needs a different sheathing and insulation build-up.
  • A gap at stone and masonry. Keep at least 1/2 in between the edge of the ZIP-R OSB and any stone or masonry it meets, such as at the foundation in FE3 (Huber manual, p. 7).
  • Cladding fasteners and the 2 in foam. The cladding manufacturer decides whether fasteners for the cedar furring and the stone lath must reach the studs through the foam or may fasten into the ZIP-R OSB alone (Huber manual, p. 23; ESR-3373, condition 5.5).
  • Structure and construction type. Huber says ZIP-R isn't meant to replace structural panels where the sheathing must resist combined wind uplift and shear (Huber manual, p. 7); ESR-3373 requires the code official to approve that use (condition 5.11). ESR-3373 covers light wood-frame walls of Type V construction (section 2.0). For the structural engineer and the code review.

How we tested

Each run covers five years, hour by hour, starting in October. NYC's cold and hot moisture design years are the main weather. Providence's cold year is a colder check. We ran north, northeast, east and southeast walls; the north and northeast walls set the results.

Indoor moisture is 2.43 lb/h, the building's busiest regular heating-season day from its occupancy schedules, held all year. Following the ASHRAE 160 default, 1% of the wind-driven rain on the wall leaks past the cladding and lands on the ZIP-R behind the cedar and behind the stone as drawn, and on the face of the mat behind the stone on the mat.

The stress tests go further. "Built wet" starts every layer at 90% relative humidity. The leak cases add 0.1% or 1% of the rain directly into the ZIP-R sheathing, as a failed seam tape or flashing would. Each run is scored by its against the .

The mold index

A rating from 0 to 6 of how much mold grows on a surface. It has no unit. We calculate it hour by hour from the temperature and humidity at each check point in the wall: it climbs while the surface is warm and damp, and falls back slowly once it dries.

0No growth
1First traces, visible only under a microscope
2Several small colonies, visible only under a microscope
3Mold visible to the eye on under 10% of the surface
4Visible mold on 10 to 50% of the surface
5Mold on more than half the surface
6Surface fully covered

Levels from the VTT mold model, which ASHRAE 160 adopts.

Why the limit is 3

ASHRAE Standard 160 requires the mold index to stay at or below 3.00. We check it at the ZIP-R sheathing and the face of the wood fiber. At 3, mold first becomes visible to the eye; below it, any growth is microscopic.

The standard rates materials by how readily they grow mold. We used its "sensitive" class, which it recommends for wood-based boards, for the ZIP-R OSB and the wood fiber.

Limits of the model

The model holds liquid water in the mat's gap instead of letting it drain. That makes some stone-on-mat failures pessimistic: the larger leak, every run with rain behind the mat and the cold-year built-wet run. The passing runs had no water in the gap.

The stone-on-mat runs place the mat directly on the ZIP-R, without the second barrier Huber requires, and put the rain leakage on the face of the mat. With that barrier in place the leakage belongs on it instead. Those cases need a rerun once the build-up is chosen.
